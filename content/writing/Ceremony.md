## LESLIE MAMON SILKO'S NEW CEREMONY

"Fragile."

"Fragile," softly chants Ku'oosh, the Indian healer, Old Grandma brings in to help a suffering Tayo. Leslie Marmon Silko tells the reader of Ceremony that Ku'oosh chooses this word, like all his words, carefully, using the power of words and stories to convey "the intricacies of a continuing process and with a strength inherent in spider webs…no word exists alone." This idea that words, myths, and ceremonies are interconnected is the backbone and heart of Silko's novel. Through the interweaving of traditional poetic storytelling throughout the body of her novel, Silko makes one point clear: stories and ceremonies are the cure to a world at war with itself. The traditional Pueblo myths and prayers that run through Ceremony do more than frame the story -- they act as the guiding framework through which Tayo's healing takes place. Through that healing, Silko argues that the survival of her people depends not only on remembering traditional stories but on retelling and reshaping them into new ones that can heal modern wounds.

In many ways, the epigraph to the story is the story itself. The first four excerpts recount, in order, the Pueblo creation myth, the importance of Ceremony, the power of Ceremony, and then a single word, "sunrise." With the creation myth, Silko signals that this novel begins as all things begin: with a story. With the second poetic excerpt, Silko introduces the idea that Ceremonies are not tangents or metaphors; they are the myths and stories themselves, made manifest in the actual world:

They aren't just entertainment.

Don't be fooled.

They are all we have, you see,

all we have to fight off

illness and death.

The third poem foreshadows and declares the solution:

The only cure

I know

is a good ceremony,

The final poem, or rather, final word, is simply "sunrise." Distinctly separated on the page, it is its own thought, its own clear, unique message. Through this final word, Silko brings her novel full circle. She ends her novel the same way she begins it, invoking the sunrise. By the end of the novel, the story has returned to the beginning, added to the collection of myth, ready to be passed on, and with that, the balance of the world has been restored:

It [The Evil] is dead for now. Sunrise,

accept this offering,

Sunrise.

Silko does not just allude to the old stories; she actively retells them. At the novel's end, Old Grandma says, "It seems like I already heard these stories before… only thing is, the names sound different." That is precisely the point. The story that Old Grandma has already heard is an amalgamation of the one Silko tells. The story the novel tells is a layering of Tayo's life with the myths of the Hummingbird and the Fly, the witches, and the twins Ma'see'wi and Ou'yu'ye'wi. Silko's genius lies in this recursion of the story-- her characters and her novel are not just living; they are re-living and all the while being retold.

Tayo and Rocky's story mimics and expands on the running theme of disillusionment with tradition found in the Pueblo folk tales Silko includes. Even before the war, the brothers drift from the old ways of life. Embarrassed by the deer ceremony, sacred even to the most devout Christian Indian, Rocky "deliberately avoided the old-time ways… he called it superstition, and he opened his textbooks to show [Old Grandma]." Tayo, too, loses sight of the

Indigenous ways: "He [too] had studied those books, and he had no reasons to believe the stories any more."

The loss of custom here is at the hands of the textbook, and in place of the old customs and traditions comes the science and facts of the textbook. Tayo and Rocky's story mirrors that of Ma'see'wi and Ou'yu'ye'wi, twin brothers who grow disillusioned with The Mother Corn Altar in favor of the false magic of A Ck'o'yo medicine man. In response, The Mother Corn Altar "took the plants and grass from them. No baby animals were born. She took the rainclouds with her." From the parallels of these stories, Silko's message rings clear: the trust in the textbook is a loss of tradition, and the loss of tradition brings drought.

Nevertheless, Tayo's story diverges as he adds to the harm against the old ways of life. Deployed overseas in Japan, the curse he lays of the rain becomes literal: "He damned the rain until the words were a chant," and back home, he realizes "he had prayed the rain away." Again Silko weaves myth between her telling of the story to illuminate the result of Tayo's act; here, the story of the Corn Woman and her sister Iktoa'ak'o'ya-Reed Woman, where Corn curses her counterpart for her indifferent laziness. In turn, wrongly cursed Reed Woman leaves, taking the rain with her, bringing drought to the land. Silko uses this myth to highlight a core message of the old tradition, that each thing has a purpose and usefulness. As Josiah plainly states, "The wind and the dust, they are part of life too…You don't swear at them." For Tayo to curse the rain is to curse the delicate balance of the world; it is this sacrilege that Tayo must heal.

The healing begins with the story. As the second poem in the epigraph states, "Their evil is mighty / but it can't stand up to our stories." The 'evil' mentioned above, in myth and Tayo's story, is always the forgetting of customs, stories, and Ceremonies. Yet, like the story of the twin brothers Ma'see'wi and Ou'yu'ye'wi, the straying from tradition is incited by the fraudulent replacement of another-- that truly is the greatest Evil --the imposter. Through myth, Silko introduces to the audience what this great Evil is. The myth follows a competition between witches in pursuit of who can create the greatest Evil, and it is the final witch, who in this contest of destruction, breathes to life the white man: "They see no life. When they look they see only objects…They fear…They destroy what they fear."

This myth collapses the novel's time; the Evil, like the folk tale, is timelessly ancient, but the problem, the Evil, is still present. After all, it is the white man who sent Tayo off to war to fight in Japan; it is the white man who created the textbook poison. Silko villainizes the white man, his upheaval of society, and his disruption to the tradition. And just as Tayo wishes to have never uttered words against the rain, the witches call for the story to be revoked. However, the witch's story is irreversible: "It can't be called back." Thus, the novel's dilemma, the question Silko attempts to answer, is not how to undo what has been done but how to survive it.

Silko makes clear that the story of Tayo's healing is intrinsically connected to the healing of the world and the elimination of drought. Having talked with the medicine man Ku'oosh, Tayo can keep down the food for the first time, and still, the drought continues. Tayo's survival, moreover his healing, requires something new. Ku'oosh admits, "There are some things we can't cure like we used to…not since the white people came." So, unlike in the folk myth of the

Hummingbird and the Fly, where the Ceremony of the Tobacco brought back the rain, Ku'oosh's traditional medicine fails to cure Tayo and the drought of his world. It is Betonie, the medicine man who blends the old traditions with the new, who guides Tayo toward healing. Betonie tells him:

But after the white people came…it became necessary to create new ceremonies… only this growth keeps the ceremonies strong.

Silko's argument is almost meta in construction. The novel becomes the new Ceremony it advocates: a fusion of the poetic Pueblo storytelling and Western prose, mythic patterns, and novelistic structure. Just as Josiah crossbreeds Mexican and Hereford cattle, "they would grow up heavy and covered with meat like Herefords, but tough too, like the Mexican cows," Silko crossbreeds literary forms to create something tough and enduring.

In some of his final remarks, Betonie says:

"They want us to believe all Evil resides with white people…But white people are only tools that the witchery manipulates; and I tell you, we can deal with white people.. because we invented white people; it was Indian witchery that made white people in the first place."

Silko delivers a final message, not just of Indian perseverance in the face of white colonialism but of perseverance in the face of inevitable change. The concept that the white man is just merely an extension of Indian witchcraft signals that other challenges may and will come. Ceremony becomes a story not just about survival but adaptation. It is a novel not only concerned with preserving the old myths but with living them, adapting them, and most importantly, continuing them --changing them to fit the need that arises. Tayo's story is not separate from the folklore surrounding it; the myths do not just reflect Tayo's experience; they are it. Through this synthesis of new and old, Silko offers the myths and stories of old, but most importantly, a new one to be adapted and carried into the future: a new Ceremony.
