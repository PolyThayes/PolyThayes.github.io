## West of Eden, In King’s Night Shift

As Lucifer plunged from the kingdom of heaven, his angel wings ripped from his body, he laughed, screaming equally with delight and malice, transforming through the air into evil incarnate. Religion has always been humanity’s tool to curb fear; to explain that which is unexplainable and therefore scary, to give reason to the cruelty of a so-called ‘good’ world. To explain such things, religious texts rely on assigning their explanatory shortfalls to the presence of ‘evil.’ As such, religious stories depict battles between good and evil, between the good and bad brother, between one's innermost desire and self rationale, between power and loyalty; always framing the believer as morally superior and the winner. Satan--the Christian representation-- is religion's evil catch-all; the killers, tempters, and manipulators are all agents of this devil. As a literary tool, Satan exists to scare followers into living a life free of sin or else suffer eternal damnation in the pits of hell. What is strikingly terrifying about him is neither his evil nor his domain, instead that he was once good; Lucifer, “Lucem ferre,” the light bringer, God's right-hand man, and trusted angel. What is truly terrifying about him, what killed the women in Salem, was the fear that innocent girls could turn, just like him; If God’s angelic creation can, so can you; This is the Luciferian Shift-- and terror sweeps across the land.

The Luciferian Shift did not end with Satan, it continued into the present bringing its terror with it. Night Shift’s “Children of the Corn” and “The Mangler” have monsters; they delve into the supernatural and fantastical. Night Shift’s “Quitter Inc.” exists more or less in reality. These three stories are horror; they have scary, gory, and unnerving elements, but at their core, they are terrifying. The terror in these three short stories is nothing new; it retells a millennium-old fear, the same one the Bible draws on. It is religious terror, the subversion of the institutions and practices of good corrupted into a tool for Evil.

“In Children of the Corn,” the Luciferian Shift applies to the religion, as Christianity itself, the source of salvation, transforms into a tool of terror. Nearly all of the page space given to contextualizing and building to the final terror occurs in what Burt observes to have once been Grace Baptist Church. In doing so, the novel makes an inherent connection between religion and ‘that which the audience should fear.’ Immediately after placing the terror in the church and thus in the realm of religion, the story subverts it. Observing the mural in the church, Burt notices that “the picture [of Christ] was crudely done but ef ective. It looked like…a pagan Christ that might slaughter his sheep for sacrifice instead of leading them;” (332-333), foreshadowing the sacrifice of the children to the corn. But moreso, highlights the contrast in the concept of religion and what the mural preaches. Plainly, “COTC” is a religious subversion because the word of the Bible, which loves equally the sinner and the saint, has been perverted into a sycophantic ideology of cult worship. Preparing to walk into the Corn to death, Malachi whispers, “I obey the word of God,” (347). Later, Ruth, pregnant with a now fatherless child, “conceived a secret hatred for the corn…But she also feared it. Out there, in the night, something walked, and it saw everything… even the secrets kept in human hearts.” (347-348). Ruth fears her religion; she fears her creator, her ‘protector. Her religion no longer leads her away from the evils of the world, but rather brings her closer. For most of history, thoughtless adherence to all rules of religion was the expectation, and to question it would be dangerous. By creating a religion with such perverse dogmas, “COTC” pulls from this dark history, manipulating the religion, warping it, until it no longer resembles the original, and unleashes it upon the small world of Gatlin.

“Quitter’s Inc.” operates under the same form of religious terror as “COTC”, through the corruption of an institution. Here, too, the religious parallel the story draws is strong; addiction, of course, is a battle with one's own demons. Morrison, then, to cast out his demons and repent, must turn to God and religion. The institution, with its promise to do good, is the religion, or really the church in the story. From his initial interaction with McCann, Morrison thinks: “My God…[and] with an inner wince. Jimmy McCann's got religion.” (264). As the story progresses, the Luciferian Shift reveals itself slowly as the tactics of Quitters Inc., become clear. The most terrifying part of the story happens before any punishment occurs: Morrison, lighting his first cigarette in days, thinks, “It tastes awful… And on the heels of that: My God, what am I doing?” (282). By the time he realizes what he’s done, it's too late, and the institution has stolen his wife from their home. In the moments after Morrison lights his cigarette, what he fears most is God’s wrath--the institution's punishment. God, all good, who sent his son to die for the wrongdoings of men, doesn’t have a retaliatory temper, and neither should his presence here on earth. Yet, the institution, the Church of the story, propogates its own evil, transforming salvation into punishment, and in doing so, falls--becoming the embodiment of the sin it seeks to cure.

These two stories share certain unifying similarities: a corrupt institution, a lone man looking for something to prove. Yet, these two stories offer an even darker side to the ‘subversion of good,’ in that the evil works. In “COTC,” Burt, slowing down after a day of running, looked around and “saw that every leaf and stalk was perfect…No yellow blight. No tattered leaves, no caterpillar eggs, no burrows, no—His eyes widened. ‘My God, there aren't any weeds!’” (344). Ultimately, both seem to question the line between good and evil. When does good become evil? If the Corn is perfect and bugless, maybe this is the true religion, perhaps this is how God has envisioned his creation. For how terrible the tactics of the institution in “Quitters Inc.” are, they work. When Morrison, twenty months clean, sees McCanns and his wife, it is only then that he realizes “The little finger on her right hand is missing.”(p. 286). Even having reckoned with the gruesome side of the institution’s practices, McCanns still recommended it to Morrison, as “Something…that changed [his] life.” (264). As far as religions go, it did well to cast out the demons from the two men. As a reader, it is unnerving to witness uncondemned evil, for in the bible, Satan fights from the shadows, never truly considered an equal opponent against good. Thus, with evil which, from the narrative sense, ‘works,’ these stories are doubly scary.

“The Mangler” stands apart from these two stories in that there is no ‘corrupted good’. There is religion, true good religion, not a perverted version like in “COTC.” Nor is there a corrupted insitution like in “Quitter’s Inc.” Brought into the story by Detective Hunton, the holy act of the exorcism becomes the key that unleashes the demonic laundry machine rather than quells it. If in “COTC” it is the practice of religion that is subverted and in “Quitters Inc.” the institution, then in “The Mangler” it is religion itself that is subverted. In that way, it stands apart from the other two in that there is no possibility of arguing that the ‘religion’ accomplished any good.

“The Mangle”r also introduces another conundrum into the concept of the Luciferian Shift, which the religious terror is based on. Unlike the other two stories, the evil in “The Mangler” results from an accident; it is an oblivious error. Each of these stories attempts to define an agent of terror, to find--as the bible does with Satan--someone to blame. Right before his death, in “COTC,” Burt describes “He Who Walks Behind the Rows” (346) as “[a] gigantic shadow…something huge, bulking up to the sky, something green with terrible red eyes the size of footballs.” (346). This quote is an apt description of a fallen angel, one on par with any good description of a demon. In the biblical canon, Satan’s only goal is opposition to God’s truth. In his pride, he wanted god’s power, the adoration from his creation, and, unable to obtain it, he resorts to turning those away from truth and righteousness. “He Who Walks Behind the Rows” fits snugly into this definition: A manipulator of truth and good, steering the people away from God’s word, demanding adoration in the form of human sacrifice, behind a tricky facade of perfect corn. “Quitter’s Inc., “too, defines a Satan. Whereas “COTC” depicts a primal beastly Satan, “Quiter’s Inc.” takes the more modern approach, depicting the suit-clad businessman who, under promises of grandeur, signs your life away. In “Quitter’s Inc.,” Satan enacts evil through the classic notion of ‘selling one's sou to the devil; ’which is precisely what Morrison does: “Donatti put a pen in his hand. He scratched his name, and Donatti signed below it.” (269) Again, like “COTC,” there exists someone who, although

framed as a helper in the story, is an agent of evil. “The Mangler,” however, has none of this, no corrupt religion, no Satan figure, and yet, the religion still manages to enact evil. The story uses the concept of a religious demon to explain the machine, but apart from that steers away from it. As many a sermon goes: religion is always there for the believer, even if it’s only in times of crisis. “The Mangler,” however, finds its terror in the fact that it's not. It is a story where demons exist, but God doesn't; where religion is Godless. Although it stands apart from the other two in so many ways, ultimately, “The Mangler” is still a story where the Devil is in control.

These stories are meant to be scary; they are sold as horrors. Each utilizes a real fear: that what we are told is ‘truly’ good is not necessarily good. What is scarier than rogue religion, or rather, what is more frightening than someone who can control your religion, cut out parts of the sacred texts, and tell you what is actually good and true? Or who promises to rid you of evil, but at a terrible cost? As Horror stories, none offer any redeeming conclusion. Burt dies, Morrison pays his bill forever under the watchful eye of the institution, and for all intents and purposes of the story, the demonic press is set free upon the small town in Maine. What these stories say about the power of corrupted institutions fighting for their own interests against good is disquieting. Essentially, that evil has the power and ability to win, that unlike Satan, evil in the world can be a worthy opponent capable of suppressing the word of God. Not only that, but that it does so by erasing the boundary between the two, evil immitating good so perfectly, man can no longer tell the difference. As these stories show, the Luciferian Shift extends beyond religion, applying itself to any sanctified institution: govervnmental, corporate, or capitalist. These stories offer no solution, and neither does the biblical inspiration. God, who loves the sinner and saint equally, offers no redemption for his dark counterpart, only fire. In Salem, there was no revocation of witchcraft except the noose. If religion is naught all that is left for man to turn to is himself and without God’s moral compass he is still alone.
