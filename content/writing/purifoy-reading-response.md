---
title: "Reading Response: Noah Purifoy and the Watts Towers Arts Center"
date: 2026-10-07
summary: "Pride, nostalgic regret, understanding, remorse: on Purifoy's art, Watts, and Baltimore."
---

I felt, while reading this chapter, the following: pride, nostalgic-regret, understanding, remorse.

The first thing that stood out to me in this reading was how biographical Jones is in her introduction to Purifoy’s art. Many of the readings we’ve done–on Warhol or Pollock, for example–skim briefly on the topic of birth and family, but Jones delves past any sort of surface-level analysis of the artist and embodies the idea that ‘the created (thing)’ is inextricably linked to the creator. Purifoy shares this idea as Jones reveals:

> In Purifoy's mind, art had to be “willfully used as a symbol through which someone becomes better”… The target of art is not only the elite viewer who would be transformed by the work— Thomas’s “cultured person”—but also the psyche of the maker.

Jones, Purifoy, and Thomas’ dwelling on the transformative power of art filled me with pride. My primary art is theater, and over the last year, while writing college essays, I attempted to find reasons to explain its benefits. “What purpose at my amateur level could my art possibly pose?” I imagine that Purifoy might agree with the voice in the back of my head, which answers that the bonding and community formed in that art, the introduction to creating, and the exploration of the self are complete purposes. I can attest that in the last decade of my exploration, my ‘psyche’ has been transformed.

With Purifoy's belief in the transformative power of art followed his work in the Watts neighborhood and the creation of the Watts Towers Arts Center. I will not claim to live in a place like the Watts neighborhood; I do not remotely know the hardships of food deserts and systemic indifference, nor can I imagine them. I will say, however, that in the almost two decades that I have lived in Baltimore City, I recognize the Watts Neighborhood in the streets and areas not too far from my Federal Hill home. A notoriously red-lined city, Baltimore is still divided along racial lines. The scars of racism appear in the boarded windows and trashed streets, in the shoddy liquor stores where grocery stores could be. New scars continue to be etched by a continued underfunding of schools (Baltimore City schools have a 32.9% literacy rate, more than double the equivalent score for maths and science) and a lack of healthcare.

Thus, Purifoy’s community art initiative struck me with an equal mix of nostalgia and regret. Not to say that Purifoy worked in a better time; indisputably, improvements have been made, nor to say that Baltimore does not have an art scene: it does. But I have not felt at any point the effort to build community around art, to make art about the people, to go back–in a way–to drawings of bulls on cave walls telling our brethren ‘this is where we are going, this is where we have come from.’ Even with similar impetus to the Watts rebellion–the Freddie Gray riots–there was not, to my knowledge, a similar art movement that followed. I feel regret in that sense, for the hurt of my city, which goes on hurting, for the poor who stay poor, and the souls unchanged by art.

In connecting the Watts rebellion and its neighborhood to Baltimore, I came to understand the “junk” presented to me as art. There is a story of overcoming which emerges from Purifoy’s art–if not, at least, perseverance. The object, turned junk, turned art, is Ab and Ex. The object represents, holds within it, and was created and curated by the expressions of a people, not just an artist. The safety pins (of Sir Watts) cling together with a defined rage in the same way that Pollock strings together his colors. I understand the art of my own city, too. Outside of my elementary school was an oxidized set of scrap metals evocative of a blue crab–I understand this now.

Remorse:

> Purifoy reminisced, “We harbored other unfulfilled dreams as we struggled with our conviction that Art was an effective tool for change. We learned since then, given the right circumstances, baseball will do the same.”<sup>72</sup>

I refuse to let go of the notion that art is not uniquely life-changing. It has changed my life, and at the very least, for Purifoy, it kept the Watts towers standing. Each of those college essays I wrote ended with the same conclusion: If nothing else, the world needs more art.
