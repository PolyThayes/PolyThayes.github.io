## Pt. 1: The Death of Ivan Illyich

You've got your passion, you've got your pride But don't you know that only fools are satisfi ed? Dream on, but don't imagine they'll all come true, ooh When will you realize Vienna waits for you? <sup>1</sup>

“Be Authentic,” everyone likes to say; being authentic killed poor Ivan Illyich. For the entire course of his death—the novel— Ivan Illyich had not been able to quell his pain for-the-sake of himself. No matter how much he tried. Yet as he lay:

1. The dying man was still screaming desperately and flailing his arms. One hand fell on the boy's head. The boy grasped it, pressed it to his lips, and began to cry. At that very moment, Ivan Ilyich fell through and saw a light.<sup>2</sup>

It is here, in this split second, that, having been falling, staring into the great black void of nothingness, he finally sees light. The flash before death is said to accompany one with a vision of their entire life, or maybe a split second of that which/who one loves most. Ivan Illyich’s flash accompanied his first few seconds of life. That is to say, before Ivan Illyich died, he had not lived; and further, as he lived, he dies.

It is fair to say that he was physically alive. And in terms of the claims of authenticity which I am about to make, he is in fact alive. Heidegger notes: “On no account, however, do the terms ‘inauthentic’ and ‘non-authentic’ signify ‘really not,’ as if in this mode of Being, Dasein were altogether to lose its Being.” <sup>3</sup> Further, I do not mean to make the claim that Ilyich didn’t live because he didn’t feel joy—or anything like that because he did feel joy. Rather, in line with that truism about ‘falling and getting back up again,’ Ivan didn’t live because he didn’t get back up again.

Heidegger’s “falling” is in the direction of “in-authenticity,” of being lost in the “they,” of which “averagness is an existential characteristic.” <sup>4</sup> Thus, the statement: ‘to be in-authentic is to be average,’ is fair, even if oversimplified. We see this in Ivan’s greatest pride and joy, his self-decorated home:

[the home] was stunning— an opinion voiced not only by himself, but by everyone else who saw the place. In actuality, it was like the homes of all people who are not really rich but want to look rich, and therefore end up looking like one another…And his place looked so much like the others that it would never have been noticed, though it all seemed quite exceptional to him.<sup>5</sup>

His life is great, to him and the people around him, yet it is so average Tolstoy should have just pulled from Heidegger himself and said Ivan: “takes pleasure as ‘they’ take pleasure; [he] reads, sees, and judges about literature and art as they see and judge [etc, etc].” <sup>67</sup>  Ivan Illyich has fallen so far into the ‘they’ that it would be impossible to tell where one ends and the other begins. I ask: ‘Do you think Ivan Illyich has an opinion of his own, a fringe, niche, weird take on life, style, anything…?

Ivan fails to get up at all. Not until his death does he take a step back and stand up in his brief moment of resoluteness.<sup>8</sup> His wife came in and:

## 2. He grieved for her.<sup>9</sup>

The grief is a sharp contrast to the hate that he had felt thus far in the novel. This contrast was fueled by a brief moment of pity, of care.

3. ‘Yes, I’m torturing them,’ he thought. ‘They feel sorry for me, but it will be better for them when I die.’ He wanted to tell them this, but lacked the strength to speak. ‘But why speak-I must do something.’

That something is die. Ivan Ilyich must die. Lacking the strength to acknowledge their suffering, to acknowledge his part in their suffering, he allows himself to die; he gives in. Death is an important part of Heideggerian philosophy, and in both Heidegger’s analysis and Tolstoy’s novel, it is often ignored as a necessary condition of the ‘they,’ for if one accepts their death, then they might live for themselves.<sup>10</sup>  I might put forth an image of Ilyich’s life as a ship, barreling ahead, moving with the tides of the ‘they. And for this conclusion of authenticity, he cannot just turn the ship; there is too much momentum. He makes his own decision to throw down the anchor—against the advice of what any ‘they’ sailor would tell him to do. The anchor hits the floor, spinning the ship, and so he faces the direction of authenticity, moving against the tides. But the suddenness of the turn is too much for the ship, and so while it is now successfully against the waves, the hull has been ripped in two, and there it sinks—falling into the water, the tide hitting the side of a ship it had not yet seen.

Ivan dies because he throws down the anchor. To understand his death is to completely reject the temperament of the ‘they,’ which would make him believe:

Caius really was mortal, and it was only right that he should die, but for him, Vanya, Ivan Illyich, with all his thoughts and feelings, it was something else again. And its simply was not possible that he should have to die. That would be too terrible.<sup>11</sup>

Rejecting this former sentiment, he uses his understanding of his own mortality to ‘care’ for his son and wife. He dies for-the-sake-of their suffering. Because he knows that their suffering is only in part for his actual dying, but more so, that his dying reminds them of their own mortality. He knows that in trying to ignore his pain, they ignore his mortality, casting them farther into the ‘they’ of ignoring their own mortality. So he dies for-the-sake-of letting them see death accepted, for-the-sake-of not casting them farther into the ‘they.’Heidegger says that:

In the light of the "for-the-sake-of-which" of one's self-chosen potentiality-for-Being, resolute Dasein frees itself for its world. Dasein's resoluteness towards itself is what first makes it possible to let the Others who are with it 'be' in their ownmost potentiality-for-Being, and to co-disclose this potentiality in the solicitude which leaps forth and liberates.<sup>12</sup>

Essentially, by defining its own ‘for-the-sake-of-which,’ Dasein frees both itself from the they, but also, in a manner, sets an example for others to do the same. Further, the example is a form of ‘care’ that opens up that world of infinite possibilities. In his final moments, he drops his anchor, accepting his mortality; turning his ship from the tides of a life lived for themself; there he sinks.

Dylan Moyar: Why do we work?

Nikhil: What do you mean

Dylan: We could just wait it out—inherit our parents' money and be ‘okay.’

Nikhil: You mean, why do we choose to do the things that will make us ‘successful?’

Dylan: Why do we choose both?

Nikhil: ‘Successful’ and…?

Dylan: ‘Successful’ and the things that keep us alive

Nikhil: Art

Dylan: Yes, art, etc.

Nikhil: I feel like I’ve encountered all the things that keep me alive on the path to being successful.

Like: ‘life is the journey,’ ‘maybe it's the friends we made along the way,’ sort of thing.

Dylan: Yeah?

Nikhil: Yeah, like I found plays and musicals by doing things ‘for the resume,’ and that’s been the greatest gift in my life.

Dylan: But that's the thing that keeps you alive. Why do you do the other stuff?

Nikhil: Like work?

Dylan: Your responsibilities. Is it that the responsibilities reinforce the fact that the things that keep you alive are the most important?

Nikhil: You mean that the path of ‘success’ is necessary because otherwise I wouldn’t know how important the things that keep me alive are?

Dylan: (nod)

Nikhil: Yeah, but also I feel that the conception I have of myself has only been changed in those moments on the path of ‘success.’ Like, I believe that if I had not had Mr. Marbury’s math class, if I had had Soden’s, I would have gotten Cum Laude, and that I would have gotten into Brown or Yale. But I wouldn’t go back and change that.

Dylan: I think you can’t accept the fact that you would.

Nikhil: That was the hardest thing I’ve done in my life. I’m resilient because I got through that— Dylan: and if you could go back and make it easier, you would, and your resilience would be defined by other failures—like not making the Traveling Men a whole bunch of times.

Nikhil: I went through that pain for something, I did the thing for some reason…

Dylan: Because that's what makes you a ‘good student.’

Nikhil: Right! Because I continued when I didn’t need to…

Dylan: If you hadn’t, though, how much better would the show have been?

Nikhil: So much better, and I would have had time to learn all my music and actually join the seniors on all the trips they invited me on. I wouldn’t have had to be so split.

Dylan: So why didn’t you quit?

I stand in my kitchen pressed against the wall by the big black void, which I know tells me that I can spend a life discovering and creating art. One filled with people who love me just as much as I love them, who challenge me, change me, and energize me. That the world is open to it and that love will be ever-present in that world, but it is heartwrenching. Because how? How can I hold peace with the fact that the love brings me so much joy, that it is only in that hug that I feel full, and know that the show will end or I will graduate, and that specific magic will close and that love along with it? To know that, if I did, I would resign myself to a life of losing the things I love, even if there is always some other one. That to be-with-others is this great, unimaginable potential loss. To lose the other and the other in you and the you in the other to time.

And I walked past the void and did the math work, which was sure to bring me something like pride or disappointment—content.
