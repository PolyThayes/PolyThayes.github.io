“Just Believe. Okay?...” “Okay.”: On the boy’s Leap of Faith in “The Road.”

If there were ever a debate on whether philosophy falls under the umbrella of science, one need only look at the vernacular these theorists use to pose and defend their hypotheses. Take, for example, Kierkegaard’s ‘Teleological suspension of the ethical,’ --a term which essentially constitutes his thesis after defining the various forms of despair man is in. A phrase that shares the same incredibly specific, though immediately ungraspable, quality as ‘Pneumonoultramicroscopicsilicovolcanoconiosis.’

Suspension of the ethical is not merely a moral suspension where an individual sets aside the ‘ethical life’; it is also,

arguably, a logical suspension. In Kierkegaard’s primary example--where Abraham ascends the mountain ready to

sacrifice his ‘only begotten son,’--Abraham rejects the ethical view which denounces murder, sacrifice, and

the-evil-inherent-in-bringing-your-son-on-a-trip-intending-to-kill-him. But he must also set aside logic and reason.

In Fear and Trembling, Kierkegaard calls what might be modernly called the mental loopholes that Abraham performs:

“[the ability] to be able to lose one's reason.” (Kierkegaard. 44.) The way Kierkegaard interprets the bible, when Abraham

sets out, “he was willing to sacrifice him if required, [but] all that time believed that God would not require Issac of

him.”--absurd! (41.)

Abraham’s leap of faith is not a single act. It is a continuously, infinitely renewed choice. One which starts the moment

“he swung his leg over the ass’s back,” and then continues every blink, breath, and step of the way. (42.) As Kierkegaard says himself, “I would have held back, even at the last minute, though not for this I would have arrived too late at Mount Moriah.” (41.) Abraham’s leap is a continued faith in God; over and over does he choose to devote himself in his willingness to give his son, and over and over does he choose to believe in God’s goodness and that It will not truly require Isaac.

In The Road, the man and boy exist in Kierkegaard’s philosophical dilemma: their starving finite bodies aimed pointedly toward death, their minds, however, on a yellow-bricked journey—as fire-carrying good guys—south to the coast. Within this context, McCarthy creates echoes of the Abrahamic story. The father, like Abraham, carts his son toward almost-certain death while preaching a promise he himself is attempting to believe. But it is the boy who most clearly embodies the position Kierkegaard describes: listening to the father almost as Abraham listens to God, setting aside reason in what can only be described as ‘A Leap of Faith.’

McCarthy conducts an experiment: placing a child in a world where the lines between mercy and death grow thin and watches whether the boy’s belief persists anyway. In doing so, he reveals--more clearly than the biblical story itself--how the Leap of Faith occurs. His story achieves that thing which makes a novel so special--the reason self-help books (in this case, philosophy books) pale in comparison to an even mediocre novel. The novel condenses and develops ideas over time without preaching them. McCarthy condenses Kierkegaard’s philosophical leap of faith into a single word.

“Okay.” (8.)

The word “Okay”(52.) means, loosely, acceptance. Not, importantly, understanding. This distinction is crucial to the novel, as there are moments when the boy does understand the man. For example, after they reach the beach:

Can I go swimming?

Swimming?

Yes.

You'll freeze your tokus off.

I know. (McCarthy. 243.)

The man has given the boy a truth, and from his past experiences--recall when the man took the boy swimming earlier in the novel--the boy knows the statement to be true. Thus, we establish that the boy can understand the man and recognize that what he says can be true.

But then there are the “Okays.” (60.)

Moments of complete acceptance from the boy without proof, without a reason to believe. The first example the novel gives--the first time McCarthy uses the word--comes when the man declares their Mount Moriah:

And we're still going south? Yes.   
So we'll be warm.   
Yes.   
Okay. (10.)

Unlike the swimming example, here the boy has no experiential reason to trust even the notion of warmth in the claim, much less that they will find it at the coast. He does not know what true warmth is. He does not know what it means to be warm outside of a lesser shiver in the company of fire. Nor does he know what lies in store for them in the south. This is the boy's Abrahamic acceptance: not knowing how it can be true, but accepting nevertheless that warmth--and the promise of the south--exist.

The boy’s resolve grows stronger as the novel progresses, even as it becomes increasingly unlikely. Abraham must have thought at every step up Mount Moriah, ‘Is this the point where God tells me good job and lets me return home with my son?’ He knows--by the absurd--through his Leap-- that it must happen; he simply does not know when. The boy must think the same way. But as the South becomes more and more unlikely a place of promise, to remain faithful, he must grow increasingly so.

The boy’s faith in the man is just as illogical as Abraham’s in God. For it is not always clear what Its plan is or whether It is true, right, or real. One of the most sound critiques of an all-good, all-powerful God is the paradox: ‘if God is all good, then he is not all powerful--for he does not stop cancer in children--and if God is all powerful, then he is not all good,

because he does not remove all cancer from all children.’-- That is, God seems inconsistent. A trend that the man continues:

You promised not to do that, the boy said.

You know what, Papa.

He poured the hot water back into the pan…

I have to watch you all the time, the boy said.

I know.

If you break little promises, you'll break big ones. That's what you said.

But I won't. (34.)

There are reasons for the boy to stop trusting the man. Regardless of the man’s benevolence, they have nothing but each other, and a promise is a promise. Yet despite this, and their hunger, and the lack of warmth at the coast, and the shooting of men, and the merciless treatment of robbers, and the presence of ships in the sea, the boy continues to believe.

Like Abraham, the boy must choose to take the Leap of Faith at every step. McCarthy paints the boy’s struggle to jump as the gap widens, making the Leap clearer and clearer.

B: There are other good guys. You said so. M: Yes.   
[…]   
B: Are there lots of them?   
M: We don't know.   
B: But some.   
M: Some. Yes.   
B: Is that true?   
M: Yes. That's true.   
B: But it might not be true.   
M: I think it's true.   
B: Okay. (184-185.)

We watch the boy struggle with his faith, struggle with the man, God, who--from the emptiness of his stomach--seems to be failing him. In this moment, the boy is rejecting the notion of the coast, of good guys, of fire, and of the man himself

When Kierkegaard posits that the last act of existentialism is the Leap of Faith, he carefully means Leap rather than merely faith itself. Should the boy--or the man, or anyone outside the novel--possess faith unquestioned, Kierkegaard would likely determine them to be in some form of despair rather than a Knight of Faith. After all, that kind of faith is simply called ‘blind faith.’

The boy must grapple with his belief. He must push against the man, question him, reject him. One may listen to the prophets and not believe them for a time before actually being able to listen.

M: You don't believe me.   
B: I believe you.   
M: Okay.   
B: I always believe you.   
M: I don't think so.   
B: Yes I do. I have to. (185.)

But ultimately, he must accept the man. The boy has just exposed the logical possibility that the man is wrong and then chooses to believe him anyway. In the final line of that exchange, he makes that explicit choice. He does not say that the man’s claims are true or destined to happen; instead, he admits that his acceptance is a necessity. In a world where all reason points only to death and starvation, the boy's faith starts to blur into hope, he must believe to continue.

There is, then, the question of what exactly “okay” (72.) means.

McCarthy provides perhaps the clearest of the word almost immediately in the novel. In fact, the definition is the second   
time he uses the word: So we'll be warm. Yes. Okay. Okay what? Nothing. Just okay. (10.)   
It is, simply “okay.” (247.) Yet as any actor worth their weight will tell you, “Okay” (93.) is one of the hardest lines   
written because of how many ways it can be said, the meaning changing with each inflection, breath, and movement: M: But we’re not dying. B: Okay. (101.)

(With a quiver in the voice and glossy eyes): Okay.

(Softly): Okay.

(Confidently, with a nod): Okay.

(With a deep breath and a shrug): Okay.

Each descriptor is valid. But McCarthy avoids adding a single one. It has long been taught that one of the hallmarks of a good novel is thorough description, yet throughout the novel, there are incredibly few ‘wrylies’ (the words playwrights attach to lines in the script usually ending in ‘ly’: slyly, happily, angrily). McCarthy leaves the way “Okay”(11.) is said in each scene up to the reader, but through its repetition: “Okay” (77.) “Okay” (127.) “Okay” (129.) “Okay” (185.) “Okay” (193.) “Okay,” (283.), he forces the reader to understand them together.

By separating the word from a predisposed emotion or meaning, McCarthy instills in his reader the ability to ask whether they, too, at each of the boy’s crossroads will take the Leap of Faith and trust the man; if we, too, could carry the fire.

Kierkegaard is one of the pioneering authors of existentialist thought. Yet his religious orientation sometimes appears at odds with existentialism’s core premise--that, in the end, existence lacks inherent meaning or purpose. For religion, afte all, asserts the opposite. Thus, while The Road serves as good ‘peer-reviewed’ evidence for Kierkegaard’s ‘Leap of Faith,’ it also serves as a rebuttal. One might ask after reading both the novel and Soren’s Fear and Trembling: ‘Does God need me?’

McCarthy’s answer seems to be: yes:

Where men can't live, Gods fare no better. (172.)

Unlike Abraham’s story, which is entirely one-sided devotion, the relationship between the man and the boy involves a continuous give-and-take. The boy questions his God in a way Abraham might even consider sacrilegious. Yet the struggle, the doubt, the despair, the absurdity in continued belief, is precisely what makes The Leap meaningful.

Whereas Kierkegaard insists that one should have faith in the (Christian) God (--however defined… and why that God?) McCarthy suggests that faith is relational, that it's a two-way Road. For the boy to have faith in the man, the man must have faith in the boy; the man must prove himself to the boy by striving to keep his promises, by failing and apologizing, by being better every day.

There are moments of faith when the boy leaps and moments when it seems rewarded. Although the man stumbled across the bunker full of food by luck, to the boy, it is proof that his faith was justified. McCarthy does not define the God of his novel beyond the man who speaks of “good guys” and “carrying the fire.” Instead--like with the tone of each okay-- he trusts the reader to put their own meaning onto those words. Thus, determining for themselves whether it's worth journeying for. When tested--do you actually believe in what you claim to believe--is your belief necessary? When you love art but are denied the role, will you keep at it? McCarthy asks these questions at the end of the world, at the very edge of reality.

There is no proof that the fire matters, no evidence that our roads lead anywhere worth reaching. There is only the choice to keep walking anyway.

And that is, simply:

“Okay.” (284.)
