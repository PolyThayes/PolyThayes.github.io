---
title: "Close Study: Cindy Sherman, Untitled (1979/1989)"
date: 2026-11-03
summary: "Cindy Sherman, John Berger, and the male gaze."
---

*Cindy Sherman, Untitled (from "The Indomitable Spirit Portfolio"), 1979/1989, Chromogenic Development print (Ektacolor)*

I preface the following by saying that from 3rd through 12th grade, I attended an all-boys school.

I say this because Sherman's photograph makes me overtly conscious of my looking. I am conscious of my spying, of her unawareness, of her sexuality.

It looks like the subject herself is watching someone else, maybe in the same way we watch her. That is, she is positioned square to me, the viewer, yet she does not notice. As John Berger writes in *Ways of Seeing* about the history of European oil painting, “Often she (the female subject) looks…out of the picture towards the one who considers himself her true lover – the spectator-owner.” This, however, is obviously not true for Sherman’s photograph. In fact, my initial gut reaction, the first narrative I attributed to this quasi-movie still, was of unrequited love. It seems that I, the viewer, look at her, the subject, and she, ignoring or indifferent, looks at someone or something else.

One of the main advertisements of all-boys schools is that they offer freedom from distraction, or at the very least, less social pressure. I wonder now, having spent some time on Williams’ campus, whether this has led me to believe that the presence of any woman is inherently distracting. In my mind, Sherman has successfully removed ownership of the female subject from the male viewer but has not made her less “nude.” Berger describes nude as the state of being naked, but not free to be oneself, and while Sherman’s subject is not naked, neither is she free to be herself. Her sexuality seems apparent to me: the way the cigarette dangles from her mouth, that classic Hollywood sex symbol; the way she holds her pinky out like some dainty, proper woman; her feet as well, in the process of painting, making herself more beautiful.

I wish, somehow, that Sherman had given her subject more autonomy, had freed her from her sexuality. Yet, her subject continues to be caught in the male gaze. Even if rejected unintentionally, her personhood is interpreted by the male viewer as working for him. The male gaze, it seems, is possessive regardless of the permission of the possessed.
